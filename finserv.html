Overview

Two projects. The same way of thinking.

Financial services is a sector where design decisions have real consequences — people's money, financial anxiety, credit records, and trust in institutions. It's also a sector where design is frequently treated as a decorative layer on top of business logic, rather than a strategic function that shapes outcomes.

The two projects that follow look, on the surface, like different problems — one a credit card application journey for a major bank, the other a self-initiated payments concept for a challenger brand. But both started in exactly the same place: understanding how people actually behave, challenging the assumptions the organisation walked in with, and designing around what mattered most to the customer rather than what suited the org chart.

Financial services has been a consistent thread throughout my career — across NatWest, RBS, Clydesdale Bank, Morgan Stanley, Deutsche Bank and M&G Prudential. The sector is simply where I've applied this approach most often; the approach itself travels wherever the problem is complex enough to need it.

Project 01 — NatWest & RBS

The problem wasn't speed. It was uncertainty.

RBS were moving away from legacy systems and needed a faster, more modern way to apply for a credit card online. Multiple teams across the bank were working on similar journeys — credit cards, current accounts, loans — with a shared design system intended to keep things consistent and reduce duplicated engineering effort.

The organisation believed application drop-off was largely caused by the length of the journey. Their solution was to make the form shorter.

Analytics supported the idea that customers were abandoning the application, but it couldn't explain why. Before changing anything, we observed people completing the journey in moderated usability sessions.

"Analytical data only tells so much of the story — and it's a very dehumanised story. It's easy to skew numbers to suit an agenda. Research puts people back in the picture."

What emerged was very different. Length was only part of the problem. Customers were also uncertain about when a credit check would take place, what impact it would have on their credit score, and whether optional features were actually mandatory. They weren't abandoning because the application took too long — they were abandoning because the experience created unnecessary uncertainty.

The Approach

Change how the organisation thinks, then change the product.

My role was Lead UX Designer on the credit card application journey, working alongside a UX writer and service designer, collaborating with journey managers and wider stakeholders. Part of the work was design. Part of it was getting a numbers-first organisation to genuinely consider the human context behind those numbers.

We worked in an agile environment — scoping and sizing based on research findings, prioritising the pages and sections that would have the most impact. The bank's existing design system meant we could iterate rapidly without rebuilding component foundations from scratch.

Responsive design tested across mobile, tablet and desktop — critical for an application journey used on all three
Design Decisions

Modular journeys and progressive disclosure.

The modular journey

During research we repeatedly watched customers work their way through screens for creating direct debits, transferring balances and adding additional cardholders. Most participants didn't want to do any of these things during the application — but because they appeared as part of the main flow, they assumed they were mandatory.

Every additional step increased cognitive load, made the application feel longer than it really was, and became another opportunity for someone to abandon the journey.

Rather than asking every customer to complete every possible task, I proposed restructuring the application into a modular experience. Core application details remained mandatory, while optional activities became opt-in modules that users could choose to complete if they were relevant to them. The application became personalised to each customer's needs instead of forcing everyone through the same journey.

The result wasn't simply a shorter form — it was a journey that respected people's time, reduced perceived effort, and produced more accurate information because users were only completing tasks that mattered to them.

The opt-in modular journey — users choose which features to set up during the application rather than being forced through every step

Small interactions can create surprisingly large barriers

One of the most valuable findings came from something analytics would never have revealed. During usability testing, we noticed participants consistently entering their surname into the middle name field.

People had developed a habit over years of filling in online forms: first name, tab, surname. Our form interrupted that mental model by placing an optional middle name field between them. Users would instinctively type their surname, realise the mistake, delete it, move backwards and re-enter the information correctly. It sounds insignificant, but it happened repeatedly.

Rather than forcing everyone to interact with an optional field, I redesigned the component so "No middle name" became the default state — anyone with a middle name could simply opt in and reveal the additional field. It removed an unnecessary interruption from the journey, improved data accuracy, and because it became a shared component, the improvement benefited multiple banking products beyond credit cards.

Progressive disclosure for the middle name field — opt-in rather than always-present, eliminating a consistent point of user error
Outcome

Removing uncertainty, not just screens.

The project wasn't about reducing the number of screens. It was about removing the uncertainty and unnecessary effort that users experienced throughout the application.

By combining behavioural research with iterative design, we replaced organisational assumptions with evidence, introduced a modular application flow, simplified complex interactions and improved the quality of information being captured. The improvements led to measurable business outcomes while creating a faster, clearer and more confident experience for customers.

Project 02 — Clydesdale Bank · Self-initiated

A challenger bank behaving like a traditional bank.

B was launched as Clydesdale Bank's answer to the growing wave of digital-first banking products. The branding was fresh, the visual identity felt modern, and the target audience was younger — but once people started using the product, the experience felt surprisingly familiar.

The interface had changed. The behaviours hadn't. The core journeys still followed the same patterns as traditional banking apps — multiple screens, confirmation steps and navigation that prioritised internal product structure over the tasks customers performed every day. If the product wanted to compete with emerging fintechs, it needed to deliver more than a new visual identity.

Designing around behaviour, not features

Before exploring concepts, we looked at how people actually used mobile banking. One pattern emerged consistently: the vast majority of sessions revolved around just two activities — checking an account balance and sending money.

Checking a balance already took seconds. Making a payment, however, involved navigating through multiple screens, selecting accounts, finding a contact, confirming details and finally authorising the payment. The most common task in the app was also one of the most time-consuming.

Rather than adding more functionality, the opportunity was to remove friction from the behaviour customers repeated every single day.

The Concept

Designing for frequency.

Instead of treating payments as a secondary feature hidden behind navigation, I explored what would happen if the experience was designed around frequency of use.

Inspired by interaction patterns already familiar through social platforms, I designed a Quick Pay experience that surfaced recently paid contacts directly on the home screen. Users could tap a contact, choose an account and complete a payment within moments. The interaction borrowed established mental models rather than inventing new ones, reducing both learning time and interaction cost.

Importantly, the concept stayed entirely within the existing design system and visual language. The exercise wasn't about creating a speculative vision of banking — it was about demonstrating how small changes to information architecture and interaction design could dramatically improve the experience without requiring the product to be rebuilt.

Quick Pay — contact avatars on the home screen, ordered by most recently paid. One tap opens the payment modal.
Iterated designs — account selection added to the modal so users control where the payment comes from

Innovation doesn't always come from introducing new technology. Often it comes from borrowing successful interaction patterns from other industries and applying them where users least expect them.

Social apps had already solved the problem of helping people quickly access the people they interact with most. Banking simply hadn't recognised the opportunity to apply the same thinking to payments.

Outcome

A proof of concept for product thinking.

Although the concept wasn't ultimately built due to shifting development priorities, it achieved its purpose. It demonstrated that meaningful innovation doesn't necessarily require entirely new products — it often comes from rethinking the journeys customers perform most frequently.

The work was well received by senior stakeholders and helped position the design team as a strategic partner capable of identifying opportunities beyond the original project brief.

More importantly, it reinforced an approach I continue to apply today: understand behaviour first, simplify the journeys that matter most, then let the interface get out of the way.

  • First of its kind in fintech at the time
  • Strong senior stakeholder reception
  • Became ongoing innovation partner
Takeaways

Same starting point, every time.

Financial products sit at the intersection of high user anxiety and high organisational caution. The combination tends to produce conservative, friction-heavy experiences — because nobody gets fired for adding a confirmation step. Getting better outcomes requires earning the trust to challenge that thinking.

Both of these projects required as much work on stakeholder alignment and organisational mindset as they did on the actual design. That's not a complaint — it's the job. And when it works, the results show up in the numbers.

Whether I'm redesigning a credit card application or reimagining a banking app, I start in exactly the same place: understanding how people behave, challenging assumptions, and designing around what matters most. Financial services is the context these two projects happened in — the approach is the same wherever I apply it.
